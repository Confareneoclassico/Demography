# Changelog

## November 2026: corrections after the resubmission

An external review of the code and a page-by-page comparison with the source
of the Song et al. model, Song, Tuan and Yu (1985), *Population Control in
China: Theory and Applications* (New York: Praeger), chapter 8, led to the
corrections below. Equation, table and page numbers refer to that book.
`song_book_check.py` documents the comparison with the book's own figures.

### Case study 1: the Song et al. model (`Song.py`, `Song_control_audit.py`)

Model formulation, now that of Program 1 (Section 8.5, pp. 262-264):

- State constraints. The book imposes three: peak population at most 1.2
  billion (8.2-9), dependency index at most 1 (8.2-10) and aging index at
  most 0.7 (8.2-11), and notes that "the three important indices listed
  above suffice" (p. 252). The aging index is the mean age of the population
  divided by life expectancy at birth (Song and Yu 1988, as reported by
  Cohen 1990, SIAM Review 32). The submitted code penalised the two ratio
  constraints as squared ratios, which are negligible beside an objective of
  order 1e17 and could never bind, and used 65+/total as the aging index.
  Violations are now measured in persons. An interim version of early
  November 2026 followed the paper's description instead (dependency ratio
  at most 0.7 and a stabilisation band in 2080); neither is in the book.
- Stationarity is the target, not a constraint. X* is the stationary
  population of 700 million implied by the model's own mortality, eqs.
  (8.2-1)-(8.2-3), in which births equal deaths. The submitted code used a
  hand-drawn step profile summing to 710.5 million.
- Performance index (8.4-2): the squared deviations of the population aged
  1..m and of the infants x_0(t) from their targets, so every age is counted
  once. The submitted code counted age 0 twice. The controls run over
  1978-2080 (Table 8.14 reports beta(2080)) and the index sums the same years.
- Timing (2.2-13), (8.4-6): the infants of year t are generated by beta(t)
  from the women of year t, x_0(t) = beta(t) sum_i (1 - mu_00) k_i h_i x_i(t),
  and reach age 1 at t+1. The 1978 infants are generated by beta(1978), the
  book's first control. The submitted code placed the births of year t at
  age 0 in year t+1, a generation interval one year longer than the book's.
  The book gives no value for the within-year infant loss mu_00, which is set
  to zero (the first-year loss is carried by the age-0 rate of Table 3.3).
- Two sexes. Women and men are projected with their own death rates (Table
  3.3) and the newborn female proportion splits births by sex, so it sets the
  female share k_i(t) of every cohort born after 1978; the 1978 shares are
  those of Table 6.1. In the submitted code the newborn female proportion
  only weighted male and female death rates, at all ages.

Data:

- Table 2.5 (fertility). Both columns had been read one row too young, and
  a Rudong value at age 39 had been invented. Rudong (1973) reports ages
  16-39 and an open 40+ rate that applies to each age 40-49; Tianjin (1978)
  reports ages 23-37. The column totals printed in the book, 1731.6 and
  1199.0, are now reproduced exactly.
- Table 6.1 (female share by age, 1978). Two transcription slips shifted
  ages 6-14 and 38-49 by one year.
- Fertility pattern normalised to sum to one, so that beta(t) is the total
  fertility rate. The submitted code extended the per-mille shares by
  nearest-neighbour fill, which inflated the effective rate by 2.2% (Rudong)
  and 2.5% (Tianjin).
- Fertility age shift: moves a share of the schedule five years older, as
  described in the paper. The submitted code read h(x+5), moving
  childbearing five years younger.
- Death rates are step functions over the abridged age groups of Table 3.3.
  Nearest-neighbour interpolation had shifted each five-year group by about
  two years.

Computation and outputs:

- The penalised problem is solved by continuation in the penalty factor
  (1e4, 1e6, ..., sampled value), each stage warm-started; the
  forward-difference gradient is supplied to L-BFGS-B (scipy 1.18 aborts on
  iterates a rounding error outside the bounds otherwise).
- The observed China series in the comparison figure is the WPP 2024
  1 July total, read from the indicators file. The hard-coded series mixed
  national and WPP figures; WPP 2024 puts the peak at 1.426 billion in 2021.
- The legacy plain design (`python Song.py`) uses a scrambled Sobol'
  sequence; the unscrambled one started at the all-minimum corner.
- New: `song_book_check.py` (see below).

What the book check shows (`results/song_book_check.json`,
`figures/song_book_check.png`):

- The compiled engine reproduces the book's matrix form of the population
  equation (2.2-13), built independently, to rounding error (1e-15).
- Table 2.5 totals reproduced: 1731.6 and 1199.0.
- With the published death rates (Table 3.3, life expectancy 65.4 years),
  replacement requires a total fertility rate of 2.31 (Rudong) or 2.32
  (Tianjin), not the 2.16 the book uses as the upper bound and calls the
  critical fertility (p. 263; "after allowing for improvements in survival",
  Cohen 1990). The stationary target then has a dependency ratio of 0.55
  and an aging index of 0.57, against the book's 0.6 and 0.55 (p. 264). With
  all death rates scaled by 0.6 (life expectancy 73.9) the target gives 0.64
  and 0.55. The improved rates are not tabulated in the book.
- Program 1 solved with the base-case inputs reproduces the book's
  prescription: fertility held between 1.1 and 1.4 to about 2000, a return
  to 2.16 within the following decade, and 2.16 to the end of the horizon
  (Table 8.14: 1.22 in 1978, 1.09 in 2000, 1.98 in 2020, 2.16 from 2040). The
  population runs below the book's, by 5% in 2000 and by 8-11% from 2020
  on, with Table 3.3 mortality. With death rates scaled by 0.6, the optimal
  control sits on the lower bound of 1 from the mid-1980s, as in Figure 8.8,
  and the population gap narrows to 3-6%. Song's published control path,
  projected through the engine with death rates scaled by 0.6, gives the
  population of Table 8.14 to within 2.2% in every reported year from 2000
  on (Tianjin pattern).
- Terminal effect. The last controls act only on the infants of the last
  years, so in some runs the prescription leaves 2.16 in 2078-2080 (below
  2.0 in 3% of runs, lowest 1.77). This is a property of the book's
  finite-horizon index; it adds 0.02 TFR-years to the median cumulative
  deficit.

### Case study 2: the world experiment (`MoMP_world.py`, `demographic_models.py`)

- Mapping from life expectancy to the proportional-hazard factor. The
  interpolation's fill values were inverted: a life-expectancy target above
  the grid's range returned the highest hazard factor instead of the
  lowest, so that very favourable mortality paths produced a mortality
  collapse. This affected 947 of 24,576 runs (3.9%), including the 2075
  minimum of 5.69 billion. The map is now monotone, checked, and raises an
  error outside its range; life expectancy is capped at [60, 92], the range
  the map covers, and the cap is disclosed.
- Initial age structure. The submitted code distributed the 2023 world
  total over ages with an invented smooth profile (65+ share 19.1% against
  10.1% in WPP 2024; 80+ share 8.0% against 2.0%). It now uses WPP 2024's
  single-age population by sex on 1 July 2023 (`Data/wpp2024_world_2023_single_age.csv`).
- Life tables. The submitted code built the baseline survival schedule
  from WPP summary indicators and counted deaths under age 5 twice (once in
  the infant and child rates, once in the "deaths before age 40" spread over
  ages 5-39), so that survival to age 40 was 0.886 instead of 0.919. Its
  life-expectancy function also summed survivorship without the half-year
  correction and overstated e0 by 0.49 years. Single-year life tables are
  now built from WPP 2024's single-age death rates by sex, with Coale-Demeny
  infant separation factors (Preston et al. 2001, Table 3.3), and reproduce
  WPP's 2023 life expectancy exactly (73.17 both sexes, 75.89 women, 70.55
  men).
- Projection step. Two-sex cohort-component projection (Preston et al.
  2001, ch. 6): survivorship L(x+1)/L(x), open interval T(100)/T(99), births
  from the average female exposure over the year, newborns = births x
  female share x L(0). The submitted step applied first-year survival twice
  (once to the births entering age 0 and again on the way to age 1) and
  projected one sex, taking women as a fixed 48.5% of every age group.
- Engine check against the WPP 2024 medium variant: the engine is run with
  WPP's medium-variant fertility and life-expectancy paths and its 2050 and
  2075 totals compared with WPP's (`engine_check` in `momp_v2_summary.json`).
- Dead code in the optimal-transport step removed.
